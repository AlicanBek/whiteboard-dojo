What is a Whiteboard Challenge?

Learn the fundamentals of whiteboard design challenges

A whiteboard challenge is a structured design exercise where you tackle a real-world design problem with specific constraints. Unlike traditional portfolio projects, whiteboard challenges mimic the problem-solving process you'll encounter in actual design roles and job interviews.

What a Prompt Looks Like

Most prompts follow the same shape: design something, for a specific product or business, to help a specific group of people. Each part narrows the problem and gives you something to ask about. A few examples from the Dojo, from easy to hard:

  • Beginner: Design a login screen for a fitness app to help busy professionals.
  • Intermediate: Design a multi-step checkout flow for an e-commerce platform to help elderly users with accessibility needs.
  • Advanced: Design a real-time collaboration system for a design tool to help distributed teams across different time zones.

Notice that none of them say what the answer should look like. That's on purpose. Part of the exercise is deciding what to design, and why.

Key Characteristics

Whiteboard challenges are typically:

  • Time-boxed: Usually completed in 20-60 minutes, creating realistic pressure similar to interviews
  • Specific but open-ended: Clear enough to understand the scope, but flexible enough to demonstrate your thinking
  • Context-rich: Include details about the user, business, and constraints to guide your approach
  • Evaluative: Focused on your process, communication, and problem-solving approach, not just the final design

Why They Matter

Whiteboard challenges are an effective way to:

  • Demonstrate your design thinking process
  • Show how you handle ambiguity and make decisions under pressure
  • Practice articulating your design reasoning
  • Develop confidence in real-world design scenarios
  • Build skills that transfer across different design domains

Where You'll Run Into Them

  • Design interviews: product, UX and interaction design roles often include a whiteboard round, usually 45 to 60 minutes, in person or over video.
  • Product manager interviews: "product sense" rounds work the same way. You get a vague problem and talk through users, goals and a solution.
  • Team practice: design teams use them to sharpen skills, onboard new people and build a shared way of talking about problems.
  • Hiring: companies use them to see how candidates think when the problem is new to everyone in the room.

Whiteboard Challenge vs. Take-Home vs. Portfolio Review

FormatTimeWhat it shows best
Whiteboard challenge20 to 60 minutes, liveHow you think, ask questions and communicate in real time
Take-home assignmentA few hours to a few daysDepth, polish and how you work on your own
Portfolio review30 to 60 minutes, presentedWhat you've shipped and how you tell the story of past work

They measure different things, which is why many hiring processes use more than one.

The Three Core Aspects

1. Understanding the Problem

Before jumping to solutions, successful designers spend time understanding the problem space. This includes asking clarifying questions, identifying constraints, and defining the actual user need.

2. Developing Your Approach

With a clear understanding of the problem, you can develop a thoughtful approach. This might involve sketching, wireframing, or discussing your mental model of the solution.

3. Communicating Your Thinking

Perhaps the most important aspect is being able to articulate your reasoning. Interviewers care as much about how you think as they do about what you produce.

How a 45-Minute Challenge Usually Goes

  1. Clarify (about 5 minutes): restate the prompt, ask who the users are, what success looks like and what constraints exist.
  2. Frame the problem (about 10 minutes): pick the user and the moment you'll focus on, and say why.
  3. Explore (about 15 minutes): sketch a flow and a few rough screens. Show more than one idea before committing.
  4. Refine (about 10 minutes): go deeper on the most important screen or step, and talk through edge cases.
  5. Wrap up (about 5 minutes): summarize what you designed, the tradeoffs you made and what you'd test next.

The split changes with the prompt, but leaving time at the end to summarize is the part people most often skip.

Common Mistakes

  • Jumping straight to screens: drawing buttons before you know who the user is.
  • Drawing in silence: the interviewer can't score thinking they can't hear.
  • Trying to solve everything: a focused answer to one part of the problem beats a shallow answer to all of it.
  • Polishing too early: neat boxes don't earn points. Clear reasoning does.
  • No ending: running out of time mid-sketch instead of stopping to sum up.

Common Questions

Do I need to be good at drawing?

No. Boxes, arrows and labels are enough. Nobody is judging your handwriting or your lines.

Can a whiteboard challenge be done remotely?

Yes, and most are these days. You share an online whiteboard over a video call and talk through it the same way you would in a room.

How can I practice on my own?

Pick a prompt, set a timer and talk out loud as if someone is listening. Running Solo Practice Sessions covers how to structure it, and our list of 50 design challenge prompts gives you plenty to work through.
